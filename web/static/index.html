    How it works

    A tip-of-the-tongue request is not a keyword query. People hedge, misremember, rule things out and chat. Desi ToT reads the request clause by clause and scores films with 22 signals combined by a learned ranker.

    1 · Clauses and their weights

    The request is split at sentence ends, commas and joining words ("but", "and I think"). Each clause gets a weight cj: 1 for plain memories, ch for hedged ones ("I think", "maybe"), cconf for confident ones ("definitely", "I clearly remember"), cctx for viewing context ("watched it on Doordarshan") and 0 for small talk ("please help, Google failed"). The weights are learned from data by cross-validated grid search.

    2 · Negation that knows its scope

    "Definitely not a comedy" rules a film out; "he can't see" describes the plot. A negation becomes an exclusion only when it is about the film itself: its genre, language, decade, cast, or "it wasn't…". "Not Race 2" or "not Dhoom 3" rules out that sequel only, never the original. Exclusions are penalised: s(d) = Σj cj·BM25(clausej, d) − μ·Σj c'j·BM25(negj, d).

    3 · Cast, characters and roles

    Every film's cast list is parsed from Wikipedia: list templates, tables and dual roles, giving actor → character → role ("Amjad Khan as Gabbar Singh, a dacoit"). Actor and character names feed the entity matcher, the role descriptions are indexed in the cast field, and results explain matches as "Amitabh Bachchan (as Jai)".

    4 · Hinglish and spelling

    "Amitab Bachan", "Sharukh", "Gabar" and "Mogembo" are mapped to the corpus spelling with a phonetic key (aa→a, ee→i, bh→b, w→v, z→j, doubled letters squeezed) and fuzzy matching. Common Hindi/Telugu words (shaadi, andha, daku, bhoot, badla) are expanded with their English meaning because plots are written in English.

    5 · Retrieval signals

    BM25F over title, lead, plot, cast, crew, genre, music and other sections; TF-IDF cosine (vector space model); a sentence-embedding model (bge-small) matching each clause to its best plot passage; Naive Bayes predictions of genre and decade used as soft filters; explicit year/language/genre/person cues; and PageRank, HITS authority and page views as popularity priors.

    6 · Learning to rank

    All 22 features are combined by a linear model trained with coordinate ascent to maximise MRR (Metzler & Croft, 2007). RankNet-style pairwise logistic regression and LambdaMART are trained for comparison. Every number on the Evaluation page comes from 5-fold cross-validation.

    7 · Explanations

    Each result shows which clauses matched which words, the best matching plot passage, what was penalised, and the contribution of each feature to the final score, so a wrong answer can be understood as well as a right one.

    8 · Memories borrowed from another film

    Being certain does not make a memory right. Every clue is checked against the films that support it: an actor against the parsed cast lists, a scene against the plots. A clue that fits another film far better than the one the rest of the description points to is flagged, the film it probably comes from is named, and the clue is withdrawn when the rest of the description clearly outweighs it. When both sides carry similar evidence, both films are shown and one question settles it.

    9 · Remakes and dubbed versions

    Remake sentences in the articles ("remade in Hindi as Kabir Singh") join the versions of one story, and dub mentions record the languages each film was dubbed into. "I watched it in Hindi" is evidence for a Hindi remake or a Hindi dub; actor clues are checked against each version's cast, and when two versions still fit, both are shown.

    10 · Scenes in the order you remember

    When you join events with "and then", "later" or "in the end", each film's best plot passage for each event is found and their order compared with yours. Films whose plot runs the other way get a small penalty, and every result shows the two passages in sequence. You can reverse the events with one click.

    11 · One question

    When the top films stay close, the system asks the single question with the highest expected information gain (decade, language, an actor, which version, or what happened next), weighted by how likely you are to know the answer. "I don't remember" is always fine.
